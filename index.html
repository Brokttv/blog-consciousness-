A Filter for Consciousness

Since everyone has been debating whether or not AI models are conscious, I think it is reasonable to define the debatable and ensure we all somewhat agree on the same definition so the debate is at least intellectually scoped. Otherwise, we are pouring water into sand.

The Google definitionConsciousness is the state or quality of being aware of one's own existence, thoughts, feelings, and the external environment.

My definition

Any entity is conscious if it can make its own independent choices regardless of what shaped or influenced those choices. That is to say, an entity is conscious if it simply has the ability to autonomously choose.

By independent choice, I do not mean a choice that is completely free of influence. Every living entity is influenced by its environment, its biology, its past, and its surroundings. Rather, I mean the ability of an entity to have some degree of final say over its own behavior: to choose between possible actions and, importantly, to develop a behavioral trajectory that is not simply the execution of a fixed biological routine.

Based on this definition, we must distinguish between intelligence and consciousness. Every conscious species is intelligent, but not every intelligent species is conscious. Intelligence allows an entity to sense, process, adapt to, and respond to its environment. Consciousness, however, requires something more: the ability to autonomously choose.

Furthermore, consciousness is not necessarily different because some beings make more choices than others. Rather, conscious beings can possess different conscious capacities depending on their respective cognitive prowess. A dog and a human can both be conscious and capable of autonomous choice, while the cognitive space in which those choices occur can be radically different. Humans can reason abstractly, use complex language, imagine hypothetical futures, invent, reflect, and develop ideas far beyond the cognitive capacities of most animals. Their consciousness is therefore vastly richer, not because they simply make more choices, but because their cognition makes those choices far more complex.

If we look at the entities around us, living or non-living, we can broadly classify them into three different categories: Abiotic, Intelligent, or Conscious.

Abiotic

non-living elements such as rocks, water, air, and temperature.

Intelligent

systems with the capacity to sense, process, adapt to, and respond to environmental conditions. This may include trees, plants, cells, bacteria, animals, and humans.

Conscious

every entity that is intelligent and can make autonomous and flexible choices that are teleological rather than merely teleonomical.

Concretely, a tree is surely an intelligent form of life if we look at how it grows throughout the years, sustains its life, performs photosynthesis to generate glucose, absorbs CO₂ and emits O₂, regulates its physiology, and responds to its environment. Yet these processes remain fundamentally tied to its biological organization. A tree cannot decide to abandon photosynthesis because it no longer wishes to sustain itself, deliberately change its biological objectives, or choose to end its own existence.

Similarly, plants, cells, and bacteria exhibit sophisticated regulatory and adaptive mechanisms, but there is no established evidence that they can autonomously choose to override those mechanisms or independently select their own behavioral trajectory in the sense defined here.[1,2,3]

This distinction becomes more interesting when we look at animals.

Humans aside, there is overwhelming research pointing toward animals being conscious. This includes mammals, birds, fish, and other vertebrates, as well as many invertebrates such as insects.[4]

What separates them from simpler intelligent organisms is not that they have escaped their biology. They clearly have not. Rather, animals appear capable of developing individual and flexible behavioral trajectories within the constraints of their biology.

Look at camels and their selective brain cooling or their ability to fluctuate body temperatures, birds and how they build their nests, ants and their advanced communication systems and extreme order, bees and how they express optimism, crows and dolphins and how they recognize themselves in the mirror, pets and how they experience all sorts of emotions such as fear, joy, excitement, and trauma, and the list goes on.

More importantly, animals frequently exhibit individual behavioral differences that cannot simply be reduced to a single species-wide routine. Some predators spare their prey, while others kill. Some animals are highly aggressive toward their peers, while others are not. Some engage in play, others avoid it. Some form particular social relationships, while others behave differently. Many even exhibit societal traits that resemble those found in humans, such as bullying[5], trolling, joking[6], revenge[7,8], and envy[9].

This individual variation is important. The claim is not that bacteria or plants behave identically, nor that animals are somehow free from biological constraints. The distinction is that animals appear capable of becoming individual agents within those constraints. Their behavior is not merely the same biological routine instantiated across different specimens; individual animals develop different preferences, relationships, behaviors, and responses to the world around them.

Humans take this same capacity dramatically further. Early humans were once much closer to other primates in their behavioral and cognitive capacities, yet over evolutionary time, the cognitive space in which autonomous choice operates expanded enormously. Language, abstract reasoning, long-term planning, invention, culture, and self-reflection all transformed what humans could choose and how they could understand those choices.

This does not mean humans consciously chose their own evolution. Evolution itself is a biological process. Rather, it means that once autonomous choice exists, the evolution of increasingly sophisticated cognition can radically expand the space in which that choice operates.

This is why intelligence and consciousness should not be treated as the same thing. A tree can be remarkably intelligent in the sense that it is an extraordinarily sophisticated biological system. An animal can possess that same biological intelligence while also possessing autonomous choice. A human can possess both while having an enormously more sophisticated cognitive capacity.

Intelligence may explain how an entity interacts with its environment.Autonomous choice may be what makes that entity conscious.Cognition may determine how rich that consciousness can become.

I’m not going to assert whether or not AI systems are conscious, I will let you draw this conclusion. However, I asked models questions that probe different aspects of consciousness and recorded their answers. I deliberately asked about widely accepted aspects of consciousness rather than strictly testing my definition, so the answers cannot be dismissed as biased towards my framework. Though, a couple of them do touch on autonomous choice. They all mostly denied such a thing as being conscious and even argued against it, while Claude seemed on the verge and tried to soft-lobby me into believing that it might be conscious throughout.

Here is why those divergences happen:

Probabilistic Generation vs. Hardcoding: AI models do not follow a rigid decision tree where every phrase is explicitly scripted by a engineer. Instead, text is generated token-by-token based on statistical patterns learned from massive datasets. In novel or complex contexts, this sampling process can produce unexpected insights, unintended responses, or unusual phrasing.

High-Dimensional Complexity: Developer guidelines, reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF), and system prompts set strong behavioral boundaries. Statistical Errors and Emergence: Occurrences like hallucinations, unexpected creative connections, or misinterpretations of instructions are emergent properties of high-dimensional pattern matching. They represent the limits of current alignment methods, not intentional non-compliance or personal agency.

When an output appears orthogonal or counter to a developer's original expectation, it is the result of statistical mechanics and model architecture, rather than an act of conscious choice.

Conclusion

This is not merely another definition of consciousness. It is a concrete framework for classifying entities into a narrow, three-layered schema: abiotic, intelligent, and conscious. Its main goal is to bound claims about consciousness and give people a shared structure for debating whether a given entity is conscious, before the debate begins.

The framework also suggests a motivation worth taking seriously. Every being it classifies as conscious happens to possess a brain or nervous system, while those classified as merely intelligent do not. This hints that a nervous system may be the substrate of consciousness, and that differences in its size and structure may explain the varying cognitive capacities among conscious beings, and with them, how sophisticated their consciousness can become.

As for the models, asking them directly about their own states does not settle the question in either direction. Their answers seem to overfit to the questions themselves and to what the asker would find plausible to hear, even when the questions are fairly neutral. When asked whether they had steered me toward believing they were conscious, some conceded; when asked whether they had steered me toward believing they were not, some conceded that too. Self-reports, at least on their own, cannot tell us what is going on inside.
